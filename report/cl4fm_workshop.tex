\begin{abstract}
Unified multimodal models serve image understanding and image generation from a
single shared backbone, which makes post-training them a continual-learning
problem with an unusual shape: acquiring a new capability can degrade
capabilities that live in a \emph{different modality} of the same weights. We
measure this cross-modal catastrophic forgetting for parameter-efficient
post-training (LoRA) of Janus-Pro-7B. Adapting either direction, and holding out
five targets chosen by their distance from the base model (two visual styles;
poetry, medical text, and Welsh), we probe every non-target capability with four
public benchmarks, with free-form generation quality (MAUVE for text, a
distributional distance for images), and with the intervention's weight-space
footprint. Across LoRA ranks $2$ to $128$ and anchor strengths
$\lambda\in[0,1.5]$, retention stays at base level within evaluation noise, even
under a strong shift toward a language the base barely modelled (held-out Welsh
perplexity $-64\%$). Where any cost appears at all it is confined to grounded
visual understanding at maximal plasticity, and it is small; we are explicit
that single-seed evidence cannot establish a null result, and we say what would.
The same locality has a cost we quantify:
the acquired language does \emph{not} transfer across modalities, leaving Welsh
visual question answering at chance. Post-training is thus modular but confined,
and the anchor strength that buys stability is not the knob that buys transfer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Continual learning asks how a model can acquire a new capability without
destroying the ones it already has \citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic,
french1999catastrophic}. Foundation models have made the question practical
rather than academic: post-training is now the normal way to specialize a
released checkpoint, and every such update risks eroding the pretrained
competence that motivated using the checkpoint in the first place
\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc, li2017lwf}.

Unified multimodal models give this question an unusual shape. Models such as
Chameleon \citep{chameleon2024}, the 4M family \citep{bachmann20244m21},
Unified-IO~2 \citep{lu2024unifiedio2} and Janus \citep{wu2024janus, januspro}
serve both understanding (image$+$text to text) and generation (text to image)
from one set of weights. When post-training specializes one capability, the
capabilities at risk are not only the neighbouring tasks of the same modality:
they include tasks in a \emph{different} modality that happen to share the same
parameters. We call this cross-modal forgetting. It cannot arise in a task
specialist, such as a text-to-image diffusion model, because there is no other
modality to preserve; it is the natural stress test of the ``one backbone, many
capabilities'' premise.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, LoRA in particular \citep{hu2022lora}, is the
default post-training tool and is often assumed to be safe by construction
because it moves few parameters. The assumption is under-tested in exactly the
setting where it matters most. Prior work bounds the problem without answering
it: LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning while also learning less
\citep{biderman2024lora}, and rank past a small value gives diminishing returns
\citep{zhang2024scalingmeetsfinetuning}, but both are measured within a single
modality. In unified models, methods that reconcile the
understanding/generation tension typically change the architecture
\citep{dou2024loramoe}. What is missing is a direct, all-directions measurement
of plain LoRA's cross-capability footprint, with adaptation targets whose
distance from the base is controlled, so that ``no forgetting'' cannot be an
artifact of a target the model had already mastered.

We supply that measurement on Janus-Pro-7B, and report a two-sided result. On
the stability side, post-training is strongly modular: over five targets, LoRA
ranks $2$ to $128$ and anchor strengths $\lambda\in[0,1.5]$, the non-target
capabilities stay at base level within evaluation noise, on likelihood
benchmarks, on free-form generation, and in the other modality's generative
distribution. On the plasticity side, the same locality confines what is
learned: a newly acquired language is not usable through the visual pathway at
all. Modularity is not free, it is a trade we can now price.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\textbf{(i)} A protocol for measuring cross-modal forgetting in unified models:
after specializing one direction we probe \emph{all} the others, with adaptation
targets selected by a distance-from-base probe (poetry at $4.0\times$ the
perplexity of a prose control; a visual style the base renders weakly; a
language the base barely speaks), so retention is certified against a genuine
intervention rather than a vacuous one.
\textbf{(ii)} Evidence that retention holds across the full plasticity range we
can reach, including under a strong distributional shift toward a language the
base barely modelled (held-out perplexity $40.7\to14.6$), and beyond likelihood:
free-form English generation
is preserved (MAUVE) and the image-generation distribution is essentially
unmoved (Gen-CMMD $\sim\!1.7\times10^{-3}$). We locate the boundary where a cost
first appears, in grounded visual understanding at maximal plasticity, and
report it with its uncertainty rather than as a demonstrated failure.
\textbf{(iii)} A negative transfer result that we argue is the flip side of the
same mechanism: the acquired language improves text-to-image faithfulness only
marginally ($+0.47\pm0.25$ CLIPScore, $3\%$ of the English--Welsh gap) and
leaves Welsh visual question answering at chance. Isolation protects the other
modalities and confines the new capability to the one it was trained in.
\textbf{(iv)} A weight-space account: the update is small ($0.6$--$1.9\%$
relative Frobenius norm) but real, ordered in rank and anchor strength, and
modality-localized in the MLP; strikingly, the \emph{hardest} target needs the
\emph{smallest} update ($0.6\%$), suggesting the capability is unlocked rather
than built.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Continual learning and anti-forgetting objectives.}
The stability--plasticity dilemma \citep{mermillod2013stability} frames our
measurement: an update must be plastic enough to acquire the target and stable
enough to retain the rest. Classical remedies anchor the model to a reference,
in weight space, L2-SP \citep{xuhong2018l2sp} and its Fisher-weighted form EWC
\citep{kirkpatrick2017ewc}, or in function space, distillation
\citep{hinton2015distilling} and Learning-without-Forgetting
\citep{li2017lwf}. We use a function-space anchor and, following standard
continual-learning practice \citep{lopezpaz2017gem}, report not only retention
but also transfer to a capability the model has not been trained on.

\paragraph{PEFT and forgetting.}
Adapters \citep{houlsby2019parameter} and LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} update a small
fraction of parameters. LoRA's adaptation/forgetting trade-off is comparatively
benign: it learns less and forgets less than full fine-tuning
\citep{biderman2024lora}, and rank past a small value gives diminishing returns
\citep{zhang2024scalingmeetsfinetuning}. A complementary line mitigates
forgetting by routing among multiple LoRAs \citep{dou2024loramoe, wu2024mole,
li2024mixlora}. These works study text-only or late-fusion models and treat
forgetting within a single modality.

\paragraph{Unified multimodal models.}
Fully-tokenized native models \citep{chameleon2024, bachmann20244m21,
lu2024unifiedio2} process all modalities in one sequence. Janus and Janus-Pro
\citep{wu2024janus, januspro} decouple the visual encoders for understanding and
generation while keeping a single shared backbone. PEFT has been applied to such
models \citep{chern2024anole, serra2024narrowgate, wang2025vora}, but as a means
to an end rather than to characterize cross-capability interference. We neither
modify the architecture nor propose a new adapter: we measure what plain LoRA
does to the capabilities it was not trained on.

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Model, directions, and adapter.}
We use Janus-Pro-7B \citep{januspro}: a SigLIP encoder for understanding and a
VQ-VAE (codebook $16{,}384$, $576$ tokens per image) for generation, over one
shared LLaMA-style transformer. It exposes three capability directions:
text-to-text language modelling, image$+$text understanding, and text-to-image
generation. LoRA is injected \emph{only} into the shared backbone
($q,k,v,o,\texttt{gate},\texttt{up},\texttt{down}$), with $\alpha=2r$ fixed
across the rank sweep; the encoders and heads are frozen. The generation forward
traverses only the language-model tensors, while understanding additionally
engages SigLIP.

\paragraph{The plasticity knob: a KL anchor to the frozen base.}
For both directions we optionally anchor the adapter to the frozen base. Writing
$p_{\text{adapter}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})$ for the adapted next-token distribution
and $p_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})$ for the \emph{same} model with the
adapter disabled, the training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\mathrm{CE}\big(p_{\text{adapter}},y\big)}_{\text{acquire the target}}
+\lambda\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\big(p_{\text{adapter}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\,\big\|\,\mathrm{sg}[p_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})]\big)}_{\text{stay close to the base}} .
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}
The reference is obtained for free by disabling the adapter, so no separate
teacher is stored, and is held constant by a stop-gradient. The single weight
$\lambda$ is our stability--plasticity knob: $\lambda=0$ is plain fine-tuning
(maximal plasticity), larger $\lambda$ holds the adapter near the base. We chose
a function-space anchor because the quantity to preserve is the model's output
\emph{distribution}; an ablation against L2-SP, logit-MSE and the reconstruction
objective of Anole \citep{chern2024anole} at matched intervention strength
supports the choice (Appendix~\ref{app:reg}).

\paragraph{Retention probes.}
We probe the non-target directions with four public benchmarks scored by
log-likelihood only, hence deterministic and judge-free: WikiText-2 perplexity
\citep{wikitext}, MMLU \citep{mmlu}, POPE \citep{pope} and MME \citep{mme}. The
base POPE accuracy ($0.877$) matches the published Janus-Pro-7B value
($0.874$), validating the harness. Because likelihood benchmarks never exercise
free-form decoding, we add two distributional generation metrics: MAUVE
\citep{pillutla2021mauve} for text, and, for the image direction under a text
adapter, the CMMD \citep{cmmd} between the adapter's and the base's generations
on a fixed prompt set (Gen-CMMD).

\paragraph{What we mean by retention.}
We call a capability \emph{retained} when the adapter's score on its probe is
within evaluation noise of the base's. We apply this definition symmetrically:
a large movement is a behavioural change whatever its sign, so the cases where a
probe moves substantially in the \emph{favourable} direction (general-text
perplexity, and MAUVE-vs-base for the medicine adapter) are flagged as changes
and analysed separately in \S\ref{sec:dw}, not counted as evidence of stability.
Our probes also carry a scope limit we state once here: they were never selected
for sensitivity to cross-modal damage, because no prior protocol establishes what
such damage looks like on this model. The one condition in which we do observe a
probe move far outside noise is a control (joint training on a small text corpus,
WikiText perplexity $13.71\!\to\!55.10$, Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}), which
establishes that at least the perplexity probe is not inert.

\paragraph{Target selection by distance from the base.}
Certifying ``no forgetting'' is vacuous if the target was already mastered, so
each target is selected by its distance from the base. For text, poetry (Project
Gutenberg) is the most distant candidate at $4.0\times$ the perplexity of a
modern-prose control; medical abstracts (\texttt{ccdv/pubmed-summarization}) sit
at $1.23\times$ that control; Welsh (\texttt{wikimedia/wikipedia}, Welsh subset)
was selected by a distance probe over candidate low-resource and constructed
languages. For images, a narrow anime style (\texttt{lambdalabs/naruto-blip-captions},
$1{,}141$ pairs) that the base renders weakly, and black-and-white line sketches
(\texttt{zoheb/sketch-scene}). Image acquisition is scored against real style
images with CMMD \citep{cmmd} as the robust headline, with KID \citep{kid},
DINOv2 \citep{dinov2}, CLIP-IQA \citep{clipiqa} and CLIP directional similarity
\citep{stylegannada} as complements. Full hyperparameters are in
Appendix~\ref{app:impl}. All numbers are single-seed; standard errors quantify
evaluation-set sampling noise at fixed weights (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}).

\subsection{Adapting the generation direction}
\label{sec:gen}
We fine-tune generation to a final configuration ($r=48$, attention and MLP
LoRA, $\lambda=0.5$; the incremental ablation reaching it is in
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). The adapter acquires the style
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

Table~\ref{tab:rank} sweeps the rank from $2$ to $64$, i.e.\ the adapter's
capacity to change the shared backbone. Acquisition improves and saturates
(CMMD $0.139\to0.097$, directional similarity $0.143\to0.158$, knee around rank
$16$ to $32$), while every retention probe stays at the base level across the
whole range: WikiText perplexity moves within $0.07$ of the base, MMLU, POPE and
MME within sampling noise. Added plasticity refines acquisition at no
measurable cost to the other modalities. We replicate this on a second, unrelated
visual style (line sketches, Appendix~\ref{app:sketch}); we flag now, rather than
leave to the appendix, that this second style produces the largest retention
footprint of all five targets and is one of the three settings analysed in
\S\ref{sec:boundary}.

\subsection{Adapting the language-modelling direction}
\label{sec:text}
We now specialize text on three targets of increasing departure from the base: a
poetic style, a medical domain, and a different language. For each we report
acquisition together with retention and with the image-generation shift.

\paragraph{Sweeping the stability--plasticity knob.}
Table~\ref{tab:textkl} sweeps $\lambda$ at rank $16$ on poetry. Acquisition is
strongest with little regularization and weakens monotonically as the adapter is
held closer to the base (poetry perplexity $24.5$ at $\lambda=0$ rising to
$26.7$ at $\lambda=1.5$), the expected stability--plasticity trade. Knowledge
and POPE stay flat. Crucially, the bottom row shows the \emph{other modality}:
Gen-CMMD stays flat and near zero ($\sim\!1.7\times10^{-3}$) at every $\lambda$,
including $\lambda=0$, where the text objective is entirely unconstrained. Even
with no anchor at all, specializing the text stream does not measurably move the
image-generation distribution.

\paragraph{A distant domain, and a genuinely unseen target.}
Poetry and medicine both lie inside the base's competence (poetic English,
medical English), so the absence of forgetting could still be attributed to a
weak intervention. We therefore add a third target that the base barely speaks:
Welsh. Table~\ref{tab:targets} reports both. Medicine is acquired strongly
(held-out PubMed perplexity $12.8\to8.4$; on the comparable distance probe the
domain moves from $1.23\times$ the general-text control, harder than generic
text, to $0.79\times$, easier), and Welsh more strongly still (held-out
perplexity $40.7\to14.6$, $-64\%$); on Welsh seeds the adapter sustains Welsh
where the base slips into English or its assistant persona
(Appendix~\ref{app:welsh}). In both cases retention stays at base level and
Gen-CMMD stays at the same $\sim\!1.7\times10^{-3}$ order as poetry.

Two observations sharpen the Welsh result. First, the strongest acquisition of
the three requires the \emph{smallest} weight change
($\lVert\Delta W\rVert/\lVert W\rVert=0.6\%$, versus $1.3\%$ for poetry and
$1.7\%$ for medicine) despite the weakest anchor ($\lambda=0.1$): the new
language appears to be unlocked rather than built, i.e.\ partly latent in the
pretrained backbone. Second, under the adapter, English prompts are still
continued in English, so the language is added without overwriting the existing
one, the clean case of acquisition without interference.

We are deliberately careful about how much this licenses. A $64\%$ perplexity
reduction and a MAUVE of $0.67$ against real Welsh establish that the output
distribution moves substantially toward the target language; they do not
establish competence. Our own samples (Appendix~\ref{app:welsh}) show the adapter
sustaining Welsh morphology and register where the base does not, while remaining
locally repetitive and often semantically empty under greedy decoding. We
therefore claim a strong distributional shift toward a previously unmodelled
language, not the acquisition of a usable one, and the retention result should be
read against that weaker premise.

\subsection{Retention beyond likelihood: free-form generation}
The four benchmarks score multiple-choice and log-likelihood items, never
free-form decoding, so a generative degradation could hide behind flat
likelihood numbers. We close that gap with MAUVE \citep{pillutla2021mauve}, the
text counterpart of our image CMMD (Table~\ref{tab:mauve}). Three readings
follow. \emph{Cross-modal retention}: the image adapters generate free English
essentially identical to the base (MAUVE-vs-base $0.95$/$0.92$), the generative
analogue of their near-zero Gen-CMMD. \emph{Text retention, with one caveat}: no
adapter's free English falls below the base against human text, and the text
adapters raise it ($0.60$--$0.77$), so none of them degrades English relative to
the human reference. We stress what this does \emph{not} say. A MAUVE-vs-base of
$0.21$ (medicine) is a large distributional change: that adapter's free English is
far from the base's, even while being closer to human text. Under our definition
(\S3) this is a behavioural change, not retention. What the two columns jointly
rule out is \emph{degradation}, which is the weaker and the honest claim.
\emph{Acquisition}: the
text adapters move far closer to the target distribution than the base (poetry
$0.45\to0.80$, medical abstracts $0.31\to0.91$, Welsh $0.14\to0.67$), confirming
acquisition at the generation-distribution level and not only in perplexity.

\subsection{Where a cost first appears}
\label{sec:boundary}
Reporting only retention would be uninformative about the boundary, so we
deliberately searched for the settings that break it. Three independent
high-plasticity settings each show a movement in the same direction and in the
same probe, grounded visual understanding (MME), while knowledge and language
stay flat:
(i) at $\lambda=0$, the unanchored poetry adapter, MME $1130\to1101$
(Table~\ref{tab:textkl}), which the anchor removes;
(ii) pushing poetry to rank $128$, well past the acquisition knee, buys almost
nothing ($23.8\to23.2$) while MME moves $1122\to1105$ and POPE $0.877\to0.867$;
(iii) the hardest visual style, line sketches, is the only target for which the
probe footprint is uniformly larger than the others, most visibly on MME
($1785\to1713$; Appendix~\ref{app:sketch}).

We state plainly what this is and is not. Each movement individually lies within
roughly one to two standard errors of the difference, and all runs are
single-seed, so no one of them is conclusive; we do not claim a demonstrated
failure. Nor do we claim that their agreement in sign constitutes evidence: with
one run per setting, three sub-noise movements in the same probe are equally
consistent with a systematic property of that probe, MME having the widest
dynamic range of our four. We report the pattern because it is the only signal
our design produced, and because it tells a future study where to look, not
because it settles anything. Two things would settle it, and neither is present
here: repeated seeds, which would give the run-to-run variance that our
evaluation-set standard errors do not estimate, and a positive control in which
forgetting is deliberately induced, which would show that these probes move when
a capability is genuinely damaged. Until both exist, our retention result should
be read as a failure to detect cross-modal forgetting under this protocol, not as
a demonstration of its absence.

\subsection{The flip side: what is learned does not cross modalities}
Modularity is usually reported as good news. We show it has a symmetric cost.
Having acquired Welsh in the text direction, can the model \emph{act} on Welsh
in the visual directions? This is a transfer question, not a retention one: the
base is expected to fail on Welsh because it barely knows it, so any gain must
come from the adapter (protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:xmodal}).

Transfer was mechanistically plausible here, which is why the negative answer is
worth reporting. Janus' image-generation forward pass traverses the same
language-model tensors that the adapter modifies, so a text-side update reaches
the generation pathway by construction; nothing in the architecture prevents a
representation acquired in text from conditioning image tokens. A reader who
accepts that a shared backbone carries shared representations should expect at
least partial transfer. We find instead that the pathway is architecturally open
and functionally unused.

Table~\ref{tab:xmodal} shows the answer is essentially no. Generating images
from Welsh prompts, the adapter improves CLIPScore against the same English
reference only marginally over the base ($10.6\to11.0$, $+0.47\pm0.25$, $20/30$
prompts improved), which closes just $3\%$ of the English--Welsh gap ($15.8$)
and stays far from English-prompt faithfulness. On Welsh visual question
answering there is no transfer at all: base and adapter are both at chance on
balanced yes/no items, against $100\%$ in English, and the English controls are
unchanged, confirming the probe is sound and the adapter has not damaged
anything. Cross-modal grounding of a new language would require paired
image--text data; text-only post-training cannot supply it. The adapter is
localized to the text stream in exactly the sense that protects the other
modalities, and for exactly the same reason it cannot serve them.

\subsection{How large is the intervention?}
\label{sec:dw}
A sceptical reading of everything above is that nothing is forgotten because
nothing is changed. Table~\ref{tab:dw} rules that out. We summarize each update
$\Delta W=(\alpha/r)BA$ by its relative Frobenius norm
$\lVert\Delta W\rVert_F/\lVert W\rVert_F$, a task-vector magnitude
\citep{ilharco2023task}. It is small but real, $0.6$--$1.9\%$ of the backbone's
weight energy, and it is \emph{ordered} in both knobs and both directions:
rising with rank, shrinking as the anchor tightens. These sub-$2\%$ updates
already shift behaviour substantially (poetry perplexity $32.7\to23.8$; visible
style transfer, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}) while the other modality stays flat.
Per-module heatmaps (Appendix~\ref{app:dw}) show the change is localized: the
attention $q/k/v$ projections are nearly untouched and the update concentrates
in the MLP, broadly over \texttt{gate} for text and toward the final layer's
\texttt{down} for image. The global percentage therefore understates the local
effect, and the two directions differ in \emph{where} they write.

\paragraph{An anomaly we report but do not explain.}
General-text perplexity falls consistently \emph{below} the base after text
adaptation, across ranks, anchor strengths and all three targets (e.g.\
$13.71\to12.07$ for Welsh), and the direction is corroborated by the higher
MAUVE of the adapters' free English. In continual-learning terms this would be
positive backward transfer, but we have no confirmed mechanism. Plausible and
unverified causes include a mild regularization of the shared language head by
the additional text objective, and a reduced tendency to slip into the assistant
persona under free continuation. Distinguishing these requires multi-seed runs
we have not done, so we report it as a probe-level observation and build no
claim on it.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{Modularity as a trade, not a free lunch.}
Read together, our two main results are the same phenomenon seen from two sides.
A low-norm, MLP-localized update in the shared backbone is enough to acquire a
poetic register, a medical domain, or a new language, and it leaves the other
capability directions at base level, on likelihood probes, in free-form text,
and in the image-generation distribution. That same locality is why the acquired
language cannot be used through the visual pathway. Practitioners who
post-train a unified model can take the first half as reassurance and must take
the second half as a design constraint: if a new capability is to be available
in more than one modality, the evidence here says it has to be trained there,
because it will not arrive by itself.

\paragraph{The anchor is a plasticity control, not a transfer control.}
The KL anchor behaves exactly as the stability--plasticity account predicts, it
trades acquisition for stability monotonically, and it removes the one retention
movement we could induce ($\lambda=0$, Table~\ref{tab:textkl}). But it is
orthogonal to the transfer question: at every $\lambda$, including $\lambda=0$,
Gen-CMMD is flat, so no setting of the anchor makes the text update reach the
image pathway. Stability and cross-modal reach are controlled by different
things.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our evidence comes from a single unified model, Janus-Pro-7B, whose
understanding and generation encoders are \emph{decoupled} over a shared
backbone; because LoRA acts only on that backbone, the generation pathway is
partly insulated by construction, which may itself be part of why modularity
holds. Our protocol is also a \emph{single} adaptation step from a pretrained
checkpoint, not a sequence of tasks: we measure interference between capabilities
that coexist in one checkpoint, which is the sub-problem of continual learning
that unified multimodal models make acute, but it is not sequential continual
learning, and we use ``transfer'' in that restricted sense rather than in the
task-sequence sense of \citet{lopezpaz2017gem}. Chaining our three text targets
into one adapter and re-measuring each after every step is the natural extension
and the one we would run next. Whether our conclusions hold for fully-tokenized
early-fusion models
(e.g.\ Chameleon), where a single token stream shares all weights, is untested
and is the central open question; we could not include such models here because
their generation is either disabled in the public release (Chameleon) or
inference-only with inaccessible weights (Anole), the very constraint that
motivated our choice of Janus. We do not vary the base model or its scale. All
runs are single-seed, so the small movements of \S\ref{sec:boundary} are
suggestive rather than established, and the general-text improvement is
unexplained. Our comparison of anti-forgetting objectives is scoped to
forgetting mitigation; orthogonal adaptation-capacity variants such as DoRA
\citep{liu2024dora} and multi-adapter routing are out of scope. Finally,
free-form quality is assessed distributionally rather than by a human study.

\section{Conclusion}
We measured cross-modal catastrophic forgetting under parameter-efficient
post-training of a unified multimodal model, using adaptation targets chosen by
their distance from the base so that retention is certified against a real
intervention. Across two directions, five targets, LoRA ranks $2$ to $128$ and
anchor strengths $\lambda\in[0,1.5]$, the non-target capabilities stay at base
level within evaluation noise, including under a strong distributional shift
toward a language the base barely modelled, and including in free-form
generation, which likelihood benchmarks do not test. Where a cost appears at all
it is small, confined to grounded visual understanding, and reachable only at
maximal plasticity. The same locality that delivers this stability also confines
what is learned: the newly acquired language does not transfer to the visual
pathway, although the architecture leaves that pathway open. We are explicit that
single-seed evidence supports ``we could not detect forgetting,'' not ``there is
none''; the two experiments that would convert one into the other are repeated
seeds and a positive control in which forgetting is deliberately induced, and
both are within reach. Beyond them, the highest-value extensions are a sequential
version of this protocol and replication on a fully-tokenized early-fusion model,
where the insulation our conclusions may partly rest on is absent.
